Hello everyone. We are Group 8. I will present Question 3: hierarchical clustering on the gender_outlier data. The lowest clustering score came from separating one extreme observation, rather than finding two meaningful groups.

STEP 2 / 5◷ 0:40

Data and Methods

SLIDE / REPORT CUEReport page 7: section 3.1

The data has 146 people and three body measurements: height, weight, and waist. We kept everyone. We did not use Gender to make the clusters. We only used it later to describe them.

First, we standardized the three measurements. Weight has a much bigger spread, so without this step, weight would control the distance.

We tried four ways to join clusters. Single linkage uses the closest two points. Complete linkage uses the farthest two points. Group average uses the average of all pairs. Ward joins the two clusters that keep the groups as tight as possible.

STEP 3 / 5◷ 0:45

Choosing the Number of Clusters

SLIDE / REPORT CUEReport page 7: section 3.2; page 9: Figure 1, DB index

To choose the number of clusters, from two to eight, we used the Davies-Bouldin index, or DB. It compares how spread out each cluster is with how far apart the clusters are. Lower is better.

Each method picks the k with its lowest DB. Single linkage picks three clusters, with a DB of 0.279. Complete picks four, with 0.616. Group average picks two, with 0.269. Ward picks eight, with 0.750. Group average with two clusters is the lowest overall.

STEP 4 / 5◷ 1:00

What the Clusters Mean

SLIDE / REPORT CUEReport page 10: Figure 2; page 8: section 3.3 cluster profiles

But look at this winner. One cluster has 145 people. The other cluster has only one person: a man with height 200, weight 130, and waist 40. So this split does not reveal two meaningful gender groups.

Single and complete linkage also split off one or two extreme people. Ward gives more detailed descriptive groups, but its eight-cluster result also includes two one-person clusters. For example, one Ward cluster has 56 people: 52 women and 4 men. On average, they are about 161 centimeters tall and weigh about 49 kilograms.

STEP 5 / 5◷ 0:40

Robustness and Conclusion

SLIDE / REPORT CUEReport pages 8–9: section 3.4; page 7: no-singleton comparison

Ward's choice of eight clusters is not stable. Eight is the largest k we tried. With a different way to measure spread, Ward picks two instead.

To conclude: by DB, the answer is group average with two clusters. But a one-person cluster has no spread, so it can make the score look good. So we should always check the cluster sizes and centers too. Among the tested splits without one-person clusters, Ward with two clusters has the lowest DB: 57 people, including 53 women, and 89 people, including 86 men. Gender was not used to create these clusters. Thank you.

Opening

Thank you. Now I will present Question 5. We used last month's indicators to forecast Container, and compared two models: multiple linear regression, or MLR, and MARS. In short, MARS predicted the test years better, but simply repeating last month's value was even better.

STEP 2 / 6◷ 0:35

Data and Split

SLIDE / REPORT CUEReport page 13: section 5.1

The data has 180 months. To forecast a month, we use the previous month's indicators rather than the current month's values, to reduce look-ahead bias. The season index uses the target month because it is a calendar factor. Actual indicator release dates still need checking.

The first month has no earlier data, so training uses February 2011 to 2020, which is 119 months, and testing uses 2021 to 2025, which is 60 months. HSI is missing for all 48 months from 2022 to 2025, so we removed it. That leaves 16 possible predictors.

STEP 3 / 6◷ 0:45

Identifying the KPIs

SLIDE / REPORT CUEReport pages 13–14: section 5.2, KPI selection

We used only the training data to find the KPIs. MLR found nine important variables, and MARS chose seven. All seven MARS variables were also chosen by MLR: GCTI, SCFI, the EU and US retail indices, plastic exports, machine imports, and the season index.

So the union is nine variables, used as the shared candidate inputs. The final MLR retained all nine, while the pruned final MARS used seven of them. We tuned MARS with expanding yearly validation inside the training period, selecting degree 2 and penalty 3.

STEP 4 / 6◷ 1:00

Performance and Plot

SLIDE / REPORT CUEReport page 14: section 5.3 performance; page 15: Figure 3

On the training data, MARS was better, with a MAPE of 4.3 percent against 5.3 for MLR. On the test data, MARS was also better on all three measures. Its MAPE was 11.0 percent, against 12.5 for MLR, and its RMSE was about 279 million, against 470 million.

But a very simple forecast, just repeating last month's value, had a MAPE of 8.6 percent. That is better than both models.

In the plot, the red line shows where the test period starts. During the 2021–22 peak, MARS followed the real values more closely, while MLR predicted about 79 percent of actual values on average from July 2021 to August 2022. Later, MARS predicted a faster decline than what actually happened after late 2022.

STEP 5 / 6◷ 0:40

Managerial Insights

SLIDE / REPORT CUEReport page 14: section 5.4

What does this mean for managers? The report has four points. Here I group them into three.

First, watch the previous month's SCFI and GCTI: both initial selection methods identified them as potential early signals. The MARS model shows a stronger association when SCFI is above about 1,198. This is useful for planning, but it is not a strict rule.

Second, read the EU and US retail indices together as indicators of consumer demand, since their fitted MLR coefficients have opposite signs and the indicators may be correlated.

Third, use the models together with last month's value, and keep extra capacity for sudden peaks, because no single forecast is reliable at the peak.

STEP 6 / 6◷ 0:20

Limits and Conclusion

SLIDE / REPORT CUEReport page 14: limits at the end of section 5.4

The report tested only a one-month lag, but economic releases may be delayed. Also, the season index's construction method is unknown, so possible future-data leakage still needs checking. To conclude, MARS had the lower test error of the two models, but last month's value was even better. So the models should support that simple forecast, and be checked again when new data comes in. Thank you.

13 prepared questions

Tap a question to reveal the prepared English answer.

01Why did you exclude Gender?Q3
We use Gender to describe the clusters. If we also used it to build them, the comparison would be circular.
02Why standardize? The question did not ask for it.Q3
It is our choice, because weight has a much bigger spread and would control the distance. It does change the result: with raw units, the chosen k changes for three of the four methods.
03Why does DB favor a one-person cluster?Q3
One point has zero spread. If it is also far from the other cluster, the DB score becomes small and looks good, even though the groups are not useful. That is why we also checked splits with no one-person clusters: Ward with two clusters separates 57 people, 53 of them women, from 89 people, 86 of them men.
04Which DB formula did you use?Q3
We measured spread as the root mean square distance to the cluster center. This is the q equals 2 setting in clusterSim. With q equals 1, only Ward changes, from eight clusters to two.
05Why ward.D2 rather than ward.D?Q3
ward.D2 uses Ward's method correctly with normal Euclidean distances, because it squares the distances inside the function.
06Do these clusters predict gender?Q3
No. This is unsupervised clustering. The tables only describe gender in each cluster. We did not build or test a classifier.
07Why use last month's indicators?Q5
Using last month's indicators avoids directly relying on current-month measurements. However, the report has not verified each indicator's actual publication date, so a real deployment must check release timing.
08Why is the season index not lagged?Q5
It is defined by the target calendar month rather than by a newly observed economic value. However, its construction is undocumented, so we must first confirm it was not estimated using future Container data.
09Why not impute HSI?Q5
HSI is missing for 48 of the 60 test months. Imputing most of the later test period would introduce assumptions we cannot validate with the report. Deleting incomplete rows instead would leave only the 12 months of 2021 for testing.
10Why did MARS win this time?Q5
During the 2021–22 surge in Container, MARS tracked the peak more closely than MLR. Its nonlinear terms include a threshold near SCFI 1,198, which could help explain the difference. But this is an interpretation, not proof of the cause.
11Why do the models lose to last month's value?Q5
Container showed strong month-to-month persistence in this test period, so using its previous value was a strong benchmark. MARS performed better than that benchmark only during the 2021 rising period, with MAPE 5.19 percent versus 6.63 percent.
12Could the season index leak future information?Q5
Possibly. The report does not explain how Container_SeasonIndex was constructed. If it used the full Container series, it could leak future information. We should confirm its provenance or estimate it using training data only.
13What are the units of Container?Q5
The source data does not specify the physical or monetary unit. We express RMSE and MAE in millions of the original Container units without assuming what those units represent. MAPE is a unit-free percentage.
